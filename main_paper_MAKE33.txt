Abstract

1

This paper proposes a multi-indicator diagnostic framework that integrates active-unit
(AU) dynamics, intrinsic-dimension (ID) estimation (TwoNN/MLE), reconstruction-error
(MSE) elbows, and PCA baselines to diagnostically interpret the effective latent dimensionality actually used in the representations learned by autoencoders (AEs) and variational
autoencoders (VAEs). Rather than determining the effective latent dimensionality as a
single unique number, the framework explicitly separates the diagnostic roles of the indicators: AU values serve as robust direct observations of the number of KL-active latent
coordinates; TwoNN/MLE ID estimates serve as numerically stable auxiliary reference estimators (stable latent-space references whose unbiasedness with respect to the true intrinsic
dimension is not guaranteed); and the AU slowdown point mknee is treated as a TwoNNguided exploratory change point. Theoretically, under a rate–distortion surrogate of the
linear-Gaussian β-VAE—an interpretive surrogate, not a theorem about trained nonlinear
VAEs—we derive the condition λ j > βτ 2 for the j-th eigendirection of the data covariance
to be activated. Experimentally, we show that in fully connected VAEs trained on MNIST,
AU values and TwoNN IDs are highly reproducible across random seeds, whereas mknee
fluctuates strongly on sparse m-grids, and that a dense grid search around the TwoNN
estimate (TwoNN-guided local refinement) stabilizes mknee at 10.3 ± 0.9—with the scope
of this stabilization limited to MNIST FC-VAEs with β = 4, a dense grid, and three seeds.
Furthermore, through Fashion-MNIST, Conv-VAE, and CIFAR-10/SVHN experiments, we
quantify the conditions under which the framework functions and the boundary conditions
under which AU/mknee diagnostics become inconclusive.

2

1. Introduction

1.1. Background: The Problem of Interpreting Effective Latent Dimensionality

27

In representation learning, autoencoders (AEs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs) [12]
are core unsupervised models that learn low-dimensional latent representations from highdimensional data [7]. The most fundamental design parameter governing the “quality” of
these latent representations is the bottleneck dimension m: if m is too small, information is
lost, whereas if it is too large, redundant dimensions arise (and, in VAEs, posterior collapse
worsens [21]).

1.2. Positioning and Contributions of This Study

44

To address this question, we propose a multi-indicator diagnostic framework built
on two pillars: intrinsic-dimension (ID) estimation and active-unit (AU) dynamics. The
novelty of the framework lies not in “uniquely determining” the effective dimensionality
with a single indicator, but in combining the indicators while making the robustness and
exploratory character of each explicit, and interpreting them diagnostically. We do not
claim that “mknee uniquely identifies an appropriate bottleneck dimension”—mknee is a
TwoNN-guided exploratory change point, and the primary readout of effective dimensionality is carried by the robust indicator (AU values) and the numerically stable auxiliary
reference estimator (TwoNN ID) (the classification and reliability of the indicators are
summarized in Table 2).
The contributions of this paper are threefold:

1.3. Two Kinds of “Dimension”: Local Intrinsic Dimension and Topological Embedding Dimension

88

In this paper, we distinguish two notions of manifold “dimension”: dID (the local
intrinsic dimension, the target of TwoNN/MLE estimation) and demb (the topological
minimal embedding dimension, the minimal dimension required for an embedding without
self-intersection) (for the Swiss Roll, demb = dID = 2; for the Torus, demb = 3 > dID = 2).
Note, however, that the main contribution of this paper is not to prove a correspondence with dID or demb , but to organize the robustness, limits, and applicability
conditions of the diagnostic indicators AU values, TwoNN ID, and mknee . The conditional
correspondence between demb and AUsat (for the Torus, Möbius strip, etc.) is positioned as
an incidental supporting observation (Section 6.5).

2.1. Representation Learning and Autoencoders

120

Bengio et al. [7] organized the theoretical framework of representation learning and
discussed the mechanism by which AEs learn the low-dimensional structure of highdimensional data based on the manifold hypothesis [6]. β-VAE [20] promotes disentangled
representations by increasing the KL-regularization weight β, which directly affects AU.
Lucas et al. [21] analyzed, within a linear-VAE framework, the conditions under which
posterior collapse (a substantial reduction of AU) occurs in ELBO minimization, and
qualitatively discussed the relationship between AU and the variance structure (effective
dimensionality) of the data. From the perspective of score matching, the DAE analysis [23]

2.2. Intrinsic-Dimension Analysis of Deep Representations

132

Pope et al. [16] showed via TwoNN estimation that the intrinsic dimension of natural
images is far lower than the pixel dimension (CIFAR-10: dID ≈ 26 at 32 × 32). Ansuini et
al. [17] quantified the patterns by which ID changes across the layers of deep networks,
documenting contraction and expansion phenomena of the representations. Valeriani et
al. [19] systematically analyzed the geometric structure of the hidden representations of
large transformer models. Birdal et al. [18] analyzed the relationship to generalization
error by integrating persistent homology with ID. Our study is complementary to these
works that “measure the ID of existing networks,” and differs in that it tracks the formation of effective dimensionality at the design stage and during the training dynamics of
AEs/VAEs.
As intrinsic-dimension estimation methods, we adopt TwoNN [8] (two-nearestneighbor distance ratios) and MLE [9] (maximum-likelihood estimation). DANCo [15] is
more precise but computationally more expensive. While UMAP [25] is widely used as a
non-linear dimensionality-reduction technique for visualization, our study focuses not on
visualization but on the quantitative interpretation of the bottleneck dimension.

133

2.3. Active Units and Posterior Collapse

148

The active-unit (AU) count [20,21] is the number of latent dimensions whose posteriormean variance exceeds a threshold δ, and it quantifies the number of dimensions in which
KL regularization is functioning effectively. Lucas et al. [21] analyzed, within a linear-VAE
framework, the conditions under which ELBO minimization induces posterior collapse,
and qualitatively discussed the relationship between AU and the eigenvalue structure
of the data. In contrast, our Theorem 1 quantifies Lucas et al.’s qualitative argument
as the closed-form threshold λ j > βτ 2 in terms of the data eigenvalues λ j , the noise τ 2 ,
and the regularization β; its novelty lies in carrying out a quantitative derivation under
the linear-Gaussian rate–distortion surrogate, using an explicit connection to Shannon’s
water-filling theorem and the KKT conditions (it also subsumes the β = 1 special case of
PPCA by Tipping & Bishop [5]). Moreover, the systematic analysis of AU growth dynamics
on real data—in particular the concept of mknee and its correspondence with effective
representational dimensionality, its limiting conditions, and stabilization strategies—is
systematically validated in this study (Theorem 1, Proposition 2, Section 6.2).

149

2.4. Geometric Autoencoders

163

Nazari et al. [14] proposed geometric autoencoders, introducing a regularizer that
makes the encoder preserve the geometric structure of the manifold. Kato et al. [13]
proposed IsometricAE, which realizes an isometric embedding by driving the pullback
metric of the decoder Jacobian toward the identity matrix. In this study, we position
IsometricAE as “an auxiliary tool that improves a precondition (isometry) for the readout
accuracy of effective dimensionality” (RQ3, experiment Exp-Syn-Iso).

3.3. Interpretive Significance of mknee

264

We now describe the mechanism by which mknee functions as an indicator of the

265

effective representation dimensionality.
In the course of minimizing the VAE’s ELBO, the KL regularization (the information
bottleneck term) deactivates surplus latent dimensions that are not needed for reconstruction (i.e., it induces posterior collapse) [20,21]. In the regime m < dˆID , the bottleneck
impedes reconstruction, so the VAE activates all available dimensions as AU (AU(m) ≈ m).
Once m exceeds roughly dˆID , surplus dimensions begin to appear and the KL regularization
exerts pressure to deactivate them, so the AU increase rate slows down. This slowdown
point mknee tends to agree with the data’s effective intrinsic dimension dˆID under specific
conditions (e.g., Fashion-MNIST), but its numerical reproducibility depends on the m grid
and on random seeds and is not guaranteed (§5.3.4, §6.1). As a framework, regardless of
whether mknee agrees, the readout of the effective dimensionality through the combination
of AU values (a robust, directly observed quantity) and the TwoNN ID (a numerically
stable auxiliary reference estimator) constitutes the central insight of this framework.

dimension and embedding dimension; its rigorous claim is restricted to the idealized
premise of “the dimension required to achieve zero reconstruction error.” Theorem 1 is
an analytical result under the linear-Gaussian rate–distortion surrogate and gives the AU
activation threshold in closed form (proof details in A). Proposition 2 is a lemma under
an idealized monotone-saturation model; note that in practical non-linear settings, errors
exceeding the upper bound can occur. Proposition 4 is not a guarantee of unbiasedness
but a sketch of sufficient conditions for conditional stability. Proposition 3 follows directly
from the definition of isometry.

Proposition 1 (Topological Lower Bound on the Bottleneck Dimension). For a smooth ddimensional manifold M ⊂ R N to be reconstructed by a continuous AE without self-intersection,
ideally achieving zero reconstruction error, a bottleneck dimension m ≥ d is necessary. (Practical
settings involving finite samples, finite capacity, and approximate reconstruction are outside the
scope of this idealization.)

311

Proof. Zero reconstruction error requires g ◦ f |M to be close to the identity map, so f |M :
M → Rm must be a continuous injection. By standard properties of the topological
embedding dimension (Hatcher [10], Chapter 2), no continuous injection exists from a
d-dimensional manifold into a Euclidean space of dimension m < d; hence m ≥ d is a
necessary condition.
This proposition justifies dˆID as a reference point for a principled lower bound on
the bottleneck dimension (in practice, we use it as a “candidate range for the effective
dimensionality”). Experiment Exp-Syn-1 confirms this: at the transition m = 1 → 2, the
MSE improves by roughly a factor of 35 on the Swiss Roll (Section 5.2).

Proposition 2 formally established the grid sensitivity, but in actual training there

6.3. Numerical Stability and Systematic Bias of the TwoNN ID: Distinguishing “Stability” from
“Accuracy”

931

Experiments Exp-Stab and Exp-Geom showed that the TwoNN ID is “numerically
stable (low variance),” but this is a claim distinct from being “unbiased (accurate) with
respect to the true intrinsic dimension.” Making this distinction explicit is important for
operating our framework. Through Proposition 4 (§4.2), we presented the semi-theoretical
framework that “preservation of neighborhood ranks (δT ) rather than isometry (ε) is the
dominant factor for TwoNN stability,” and demonstrated it on real data across multiple
models in experiment Exp-Real-Geom (§5.10).
What numerical stability means: In experiment Exp-Geom (MNIST AE m-sweep), for
m ≥ 48 ≈ 5dˆID the TwoNN ID converges stably within the range 10 ± 0.5, and even against
the sharp increase of κ at m = 96–128 (9.94 → 37.0) the variation remains small (§5.2.4,
Table A2). That is, TwoNN is stable in the sense that “the reference AE outputs nearly the
same value even when m is changed.”
Possibility of systematic bias: However, when the reference AE embeds the space
with distortion (κ ≫ 1), TwoNN estimates “the local degrees of freedom of the reference
AE’s latent manifold,” which may deviate systematically from the intrinsic dimension of the
true data manifold. This is not inconsistent with stably returning a “particular value”—it
is, so to speak, a “stably biased estimate.” We therefore treat TwoNN not as an unbiased
estimator of the true dID but as an auxiliary reference estimator.
Grounds for dˆID ≈ 10–12 on MNIST: As independent evidence reinforcing the meaning of the TwoNN estimate, the following multiple observations are mutually consistent:

7. Conclusions

1103

In this paper, we proposed a multi-indicator framework for diagnosing the effective
latent dimensionality of AEs/VAEs. The central contribution of this paper is a clear role
classification of AU values (robust indicator), TwoNN ID (numerically stable auxiliary
reference estimator), and mknee (exploratory indicator), quantifying the reliability and
applicability conditions of each indicator from both the experimental and theoretical sides
(Table 2). On the theoretical side, we derived the AU activation threshold λ j > βτ 2
under the linear-Gaussian rate–distortion surrogate from the water-filling theorem and
the KKT conditions (Theorem 1), and presented a grid-sensitivity upper bound for mknee
(Proposition 2) together with an ensemble stabilization strategy (Section 6.2). On the
experimental side, we confirmed the high reproducibility of AU values (σ ≤ 0.5) and
TwoNN ID (10.03 ± 0.12) on MNIST (3 seeds), and stabilized mknee = 10.3 ± 0.9 under
a dense grid placed around the TwoNN estimate, with independent initialization and
multiple seeds (Exp-Mnist-Dense-300; Section 5.3.6). This stabilization is a TwoNN-guided
local refinement, and its verified scope is limited to MNIST FC-VAEs with β = 4, a
dense grid, and three seeds—we do not recommend general use of mknee without stating
these conditions. We confirmed the same tendency on Fashion-MNIST with a single seed
(Section 5.4). Through the real-data geometry comparison experiment (Exp-Real-Geom,
Section 5.10), we supported the claim that “m ≫ dˆID , rather than isometry improvement,
is the dominant condition for TwoNN stability” by a four-model comparison on MNIST
(Table 21) and a qualitative confirmation on Fashion-MNIST (Appendix E) (with theoretical
grounding in Proposition 4). The TwoNN ID is a stable latent-space reference, and its
unbiasedness with respect to the true intrinsic dimension of the input distribution is not
guaranteed (Section 6.3).

Sections 5.3.4, 5.3.6, 5.5.1, and 5.11).

(Experiment Exp-Tau-Cross)
Rationale for Choosing τknee = 0.25

1241
1242
1243
1244

1245
1246
1247

Theoretical rationale: By Theorem 1, the raw growth rate s(m) = ∆AU/∆m of AU(m)
(Equation (2)) exhibits a theoretical step: s ≈ 1 for m < d∗ ( β) (before saturation; each new
dimension becomes fully active) and s → 0 for m > d∗ ( β) (after saturation; additional
dimensions remain inactive). Because the actual AU takes discrete integer values, this
transition occurs smoothly (or discretely). The value τknee = 0.25 corresponds to “at most
1/4 of the maximum growth rate” and, as a midpoint between the pre-saturation regime
(s ≈ 1) and the post-saturation regime (s ≈ 0), constitutes a well-balanced threshold that
captures the onset of the “clearly slowed” region.
Approximating the normalized growth rate ρ(m) (Equation (3)) by an exponential
saturation model gives
ρ(m) ≈ exp(−γ(m − dID )).
(A12)
We define the slowdown point as the point where ρ(m) < τknee = 0.25 (below 25% after
normalization) (Equation (4)).
As a more rigorous threshold choice, using the point where the discrete second
difference of AU(m) is maximized (the maximum-curvature point) is theoretically motivated, but due to the discreteness of AU it is often numerically unstable on real data. The
value τ = 0.25 serves as a stable surrogate near this maximum-curvature point (see the
Exp-Tau-Cross sensitivity analysis).
Empirical rationale: On MNIST (the single-seed Exp-Mnist-Base experiment), mknee =
12 (stable) over the entire range τ ∈ [0.1, 0.5]. On Fashion-MNIST, τ ≈ 0.25 exhibits
boundary sensitivity (Table A1). The existence of boundary sensitivity suggests that the
gradient transition of the AU(m) curve occurs at values near τ = 0.25, which in turn
corroborates the “discriminative power” of this threshold.

Appendix G. Sensitivity Analysis of the AU Threshold δ
On Swiss Roll and Torus, we confirmed that the AU patterns coincide exactly for
δ ∈ {0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1} (agreement across all combinations of m and δ). Because the
variance of active dimensions (O(1)) and that of inactive dimensions (O(10−4 ) or below)
differ by more than two orders of magnitude, the result is robust to the choice of δ.
